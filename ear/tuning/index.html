Tuning systems

How the twelve notes of the octave were tuned from the Middle Ages to today. Listen to scales and chords in each system, compare them, sing against them, and learn to tune them yourself.

Pure fifths (3:2) and pure major thirds (5:4) cannot live together on a keyboard. Twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma; four pure fifths overshoot a pure major third (plus two octaves) by the syntonic comma. Every tuning system on this page is a decision about where to put these small leftovers – into one unusable “wolf” fifth, evenly into all fifths, or unevenly, so that some keys are sweeter than others.

The Euler lattice (Tonnetz)

In his Tentamen novae theoriae musicae (1739) Leonhard Euler drew the notes of pure intonation as a network: one direction multiplies by 3 (pure fifths), the other by 5 (pure major thirds). In the 19th century Arthur von Oettingen and Hugo Riemann turned it into the Tonnetz, and today neo-Riemannian theory uses it to describe how chords move: flipping a triangle over one of its edges keeps two notes and moves the third by a step.

Drawn with triangles, every upward triangle is a pure major triad and every downward triangle a pure minor triad. The position of a note matters: the E reached by a third from C (5:4) is a syntonic comma lower than the E reached by four fifths (81:64). The small marks show this: −S means a syntonic comma lower than the Pythagorean note of the same name.

Two dimensions: fifths and thirds

Tap a note to hear it; tap a triangle to hear its chord. Your path is drawn, so you can see where you drift.

Three dimensions: the harmonic seventh

Euler himself suggested in 1764 that the dominant seventh chord owes its sound to the seventh harmonic. Add 7:4 as a third axis and the plane becomes a space; in the 20th century Harry Partch, Ben Johnston and James Tenney explored it. The harmonic seventh (968.8 c) is 31 c lower than the equal-tempered minor seventh; together with the major triad it makes the chord 4:5:6:7, which barbershop quartets tune by ear. The mark −Z stands for the septimal comma 64:63 (27.3 c).

Four dimensions: the eleventh harmonic

A fourth axis cannot be built in our space, but it can be imagined – the way a cube is drawn as two squares joined at the corners. Here the whole 3D lattice is shown three times, each copy one step of 11:8 away. The interval 11:8 (551.3 c) lies almost exactly between the fourth and the tritone; Partch built his 43-note scale on this 11-limit space. +U stands for the undecimal comma 33:32 (53.3 c). Every further prime – 13, 17, 19 … – adds another dimension: the harmonic series as a space of ever more directions, which Tenney called harmonic space.

Marchetto of Padua: the whole tone in five parts

Long before temperaments, Marchetto of Padua described an extraordinary way of placing the sharps. In his Lucidarium in arte musicae planae (1317–18) and Pomerium in arte musicae mensuratae (1318–19) he divides the whole tone into five parts, which he calls dieses. A semitone can be enharmonic (two dieses), diatonic (three) or chromatic (four).

When a voice is raised to lead into a perfect consonance – the sixth widening to the octave (A–F♯ → G–G) or the third widening to the fifth (A–C♯ → G–D) – Marchetto takes the large chromatic semitone. The sharp then stands only one diesis (about a fifth of a tone, roughly 40–50 cents) below the note it resolves to. The imperfect consonance becomes very wide and very tense, and its resolution all the more satisfying: the opposite of meantone, where sharps are low.

How literally Marchetto meant his numbers is debated. John Rahn (Music Theory Online, 1998) shows how they can be realised on the monochord and finds that listeners easily hear the difference.
